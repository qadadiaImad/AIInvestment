# The Trading-Brand Playbook: Rules Extracted From 25+ Skool, YouTube, and Short-Form Trading/Investing Brands

> **Provenance.** Researched 2026-07-15. This document cross-compares four prior segment reports (Skool-native communities; YouTube-first educators; short-form/TikTok creators; method-brand ecosystems + a regulatory failure file), each built by extensive WebSearch. WebFetch was largely blocked by the research proxy for all four, so every finding here rests on **search-result snippets**, not direct page reads. Every member count, price, revenue, or subscriber figure is **reported** (self-reported by a brand, or a third-party estimate — sometimes conflicting across sources) unless marked **fact** (a directly observable live-listed price, a company-published/CPA-audited figure, a regulator press release, or a court record). Nothing below is invented; where the source research flagged a number as thin, single-sourced, or contested, that flag is carried forward rather than resolved by guessing. **Educational/competitive research only — not legal, compliance, tax, or investment advice**, and not an endorsement or condemnation of any named individual or brand.
>
> This playbook is written to sit alongside `docs/skool-monetization-roadmap.md` (the owner's existing execution plan) — it doesn't replace that roadmap's sequencing, it supplies cross-brand evidence for *why* its choices (free Skool first, the daily brief as the retention weapon, the stack framing as the niche statement, session recaps as Community B's hook) match what independently wins across 25+ comparable brands, and it flags where the evidence argues for going further.

## How to use this document

1. **Part I — 15 ranked rules.** Ordered by strength of cross-brand evidence and relevance to the owner's two communities. Each rule: a one-line principle, the mechanism, named evidence (three-plus brands, counterexamples noted), and one application labeled **"For the Engineer's Desk."**
2. **Part II — The anti-rules**, from the Failure File's case ledger — what never to do, each tied to a named case.
3. **Part III — The composite playbook**, one build sequence (identity → free content engine → named method → free community → paid ladder → retention rituals) written against the owner's actual assets.
4. **Part IV — Named method opportunity**, three candidate vocabularies the owner could coin.
5. **Part V — Sources appendix**, every URL cited across the four underlying reports, labeled fact/reported.

## Part I — The 15 rules, ranked

### 1. Identity is the moat; the trading content underneath it is commodity knowledge

**Mechanism:** Price action, ORB setups, forex swing entries, and options alerts are taught free by a dozen creators; what varies is who the material is aimed at and how seen/safe that audience feels. A sharp identity filter converts strangers into members faster than strategy alone, and it is provably portable — prove it in one segment and re-skin it into an adjacent one without touching the underlying curriculum.

**Evidence:**
- **Trading for Women** built its moat on "safe space, no judgment" atop generic forex/prop-firm content, then forked the same system into "Forex For Men VIP" once the filter was proven.
- **Bear Bull Traders** (Andrew Aziz) filters explicitly for "mid-career professionals building a second income" — the closest identity match to the owner's own audience found anywhere in this research.
- **TJR** ("-$2,000 to $10M+" — reported/disputed; Part V's own source list flags this exact figure "reported/disputed," a label this bullet omitted) and **Ricky Gutierrez** ("youngest millionaire in Gilbert, AZ" — reported, an unverifiable superlative with no checkable registry behind it) both filter for underdog/aspirational beginners over credentialed insiders.
- Counterexample — **Just Simple Trading** has almost no identity filter beyond "beginner chasing big numbers" and is the thinnest, least-differentiated brand in the Skool set.

**Survivorship-bias check [added by adversarial audit]:** would a brand that later collapsed or drew an enforcement action also satisfy this rule? Yes, easily. RagingBull's Jason Bond/Jeffrey Bishop/Kyle Dennis ran a sharp, confident-insider identity filter and drew a $137M FTC action (Anti-Rule 3); IM Academy's recruiting culture ran an extremely strong belonging/identity filter and drew a $795.8M judgment (Anti-Rule 2); ICT-adjacent communities have one of the sharpest identity filters in this entire research set and are this document's own named example of "cult-like" orthodoxy (Rule 15). The test: pull the enforcement-action cases in Part II and check whether any of them lacked a sharp identity filter — none did. A strong filter predicts *conversion speed*, not legitimacy or survival; what actually separates the fifteen positively-cited brands from the Part II cases is disclosure and compensation structure, not identity sharpness. Read Rule 1 as necessary, not sufficient.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** No brand in this research runs "engineer who couldn't afford Bloomberg, built his own terminal" — it is genuinely rare. Treat Technicals Desk as a TFW-style *fork* of the same engine, not a copy-paste: prove the AI Stack filter converts before assuming the day-job-trader filter converts the same way.

### 2. A stamped, reproducible data pipeline is the single largest open credibility gap in this market — and it's already built

**Mechanism:** Across every brand studied, "proof" almost always means a self-controlled screenshot, a journaling-app dashboard, a live screen-share, or a claimed net worth — all trivially fakeable or cherry-picked. Only two brands built anything structurally harder to fake, and the gap between "self-reported" and "audited/reproducible" is the sharpest, most recurring criticism across all four segments.

**Evidence:**
- **Warrior Trading**'s quarterly CPA-reviewed P&L ($18,810,638 cumulative, fact, company-published) is "the standout transparency case" — yet audited *data* didn't prevent an FTC action over how the number was *marketed* (Anti-Rule 1); the two are separate problems.
- **Timothy Sykes**' Profit.ly is a platform-level, trade-by-trade public log, called a differentiator "competitors couldn't easily copy."
- **Umar Ashraf**'s "$20K into $18M" claim (reported, self-controlled — no third-party audit trail), backed only by journaling-software screenshots and live logins, is flagged as the sharpest version of the self-controlled-proof critique in the short-form set.
- **TJR**, **Ricky Gutierrez**, and **ICT** all face the identical "does the guru still actually trade or just teach" question with zero third-party audit trail among them.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** This is the terminal's actual commercial purpose, not just a research tool. Every capture card and carousel slide already inherits this repo's `retrieved_at`/`source` stamping discipline internally — make that provenance stamp a **visible, branded part of the card design**, not just a data-hygiene practice. That single choice beats any screen-share or lifestyle flex in this entire research set.

### 3. Paywall proximity and structure, not information — give the whole method away free

**Mechanism:** High-reach brands give away a *complete* asset, not a teaser: a full course, a full vocabulary, a full framework. What stays paywalled is almost never "the missing information" — it's live access to the teacher, sequencing/accountability, a community to ask questions in, or a shot at being personally selected. Treating free as a complete product, not a trailer, is what lets these brands convert without feeling like bait-and-switch.

**Evidence:**
- **Steven Hart / The Trading Channel** posts a full beginner-to-advanced course free on YouTube before ever pitching the $39.99/mo EAP subscription.
- **ICT** reversed the standard order entirely: closed a $20,000+ mentorship and made the whole method permanently free, monetizing reputation/ad revenue instead.
- **Jason Graystone**'s brand identity is literally named "Always Free"; **Rayner Teo** backs the same instinct with 845 free videos and 35+ free guides before any paid pitch.
- Counterexample — **Freedom Team Trading / The Vault** gates its backend mentorship price behind an application call rather than publishing it, which reads "evasive to price-sensitive shoppers" despite a generous free front end.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** The daily brief and a rotating set of capture-card breakdowns (one AI-stack layer read, one session-level walkthrough) should be the complete, ungated product every day. The paywall belongs on live session commentary, the terminal's full historical/archive layer, and direct Q&A — never on "today's numbers."

### 4. The villain is always a system, never a name — and that discipline keeps the content engine renewable

**Mechanism:** Naming a rival creates a finite, adversarial content well that eventually invites a legal or reputational rebuttal; naming a structural enemy (gatekeeping, hype culture, an opaque incentive) creates a well that never runs dry, because new examples keep appearing, and it carries no defamation exposure. Every brand studied across all four segments independently converged on this choice.

**Evidence:**
- **Trading for Women**'s foil is generic "red flags in trading communities," never a competitor.
- **Bear Bull Traders** positions against "opinions and social media hype," backed by published research rather than a rival's name.
- **ICT**'s entire pedagogy is retail-vs.-"the institutions" — a target that can never sue back.
- **Humbled Trader** runs a dedicated "Exposing Trading Gurus" series that critiques the *archetype*, not one person, and **Ricky Gutierrez** does the same against "gurus who only show green trades."

**Caveat [added by adversarial audit]:** Anti-Rule 9 later notes that *no* brand in the Failure File — including every enforcement-action case — ever named a real rival either; adoption of this discipline is universal, not differentiating, which the Mechanism's "every brand studied... independently converged" line already half-admits without drawing the conclusion. That makes this rule necessary hygiene rather than a growth lever: it explains why brands don't get sued or don't hand a rival a rebuttal moment, not why some brands outgrow others. Don't rank it by "strength of cross-brand evidence" as if universal adoption were itself the strong signal — zero variance across outcomes can't be evidence of what drives variance in outcomes.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** This is already the owner's chosen framing, and the research confirms it's the market *norm*, not a differentiator — the discipline is never letting a specific screenshot of a specific person's course or alert service appear in a card, even as a "here's what not to do" example. Keep every critique aimed at "the paywalled-Bloomberg-terminal model," never a named seller.

### 5. A named, proprietary vocabulary is IP that compounds for free once it leaks into the commons

**Mechanism:** A memorable set of coined terms lets a teacher label price action or a fundamental pattern in a way no generic term can; once other educators adopt those terms unattributed, every one of their videos becomes free distribution that still traces credit to the coiner. The catch: an *unfalsifiable* vocabulary (one that can label anything as confirmation after the fact) draws the same criticism it draws adoption — terms need to resolve to something checkable, not just a compelling story.

**Evidence:**
- **ICT**'s "order block," "fair value gap," and "kill zone" genericized into "Smart Money Concepts," taught by dozens of unaffiliated academies with zero royalty back — and **TJR** (2M+ TikTok) explicitly builds his own strategy content on "SMC/ICT-influenced order blocks," a real cross-segment example of the vocabulary compounding a different creator's brand.
- **Wyckoff**'s vocabulary underwent a documented "Wyckoff 2.0" revival after 2018, now shipped as native chart templates in TradingView/Thinkorswim.
- **Elliott Wave**'s terminology spawned entirely independent competing brands (elliottwave-forecast.com) with no relationship to Elliott Wave International.
- Counter-lesson: ICT and Elliott Wave draw the *identical* academic critique (Mandelbrot on Elliott Wave, echoed almost word-for-word by ICT's critics) that the vocabulary can "fit any segment of market history" — narrative vocabulary is a double-edged sword.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** Coin vocabulary that resolves to an actual computed field in the terminal's schema, not a subjective chart read — see Part IV for candidates built from the stack-layer taxonomy already in `references/investing-brief.md` and the session-level data already planned for Technicals Desk.

### 6. The synchronous live ritual is the one retention mechanic a static course or PDF can never replicate

**Mechanism:** Recorded, async-replayable content is universal and necessary, but every brand that sustains a paying community anchors it with a real-time, fixed-schedule event members show up for — a scheduled appointment converts "I have access to content" into "I am part of something happening right now," which is what actually prevents churn.

**Evidence:**
- **Trading for Women** rotates live sessions across Tokyo/London/NY time zones specifically so every member's day job has a window.
- **Mike Trades Daily** live-trades every NYC market open as the anchor of an otherwise single-strategy, mid-sized community.
- **Warrior Trading**, **Humbled Trader**, **The Trading Cafe**, **Bear Bull Traders** ("Market Replay"), **SMB Capital** (live trade reviews), and **Brad Goh's 1% Club** (daily live sessions) all independently converged on the same mechanic.
- Counterexample — **Adam Khoo's Wealth Academy** substitutes lifetime video-library access for a live component and is noted as "less chat-room-centric," a structurally different and less-evidenced-as-sticky model that needed 20+ years of pre-existing fame to sustain.

**Caveat [added by adversarial audit]:** live delivery is not itself protective — it is also the exact format behind two of Part II's ten anti-rules. Online Trading Academy's negotiate-the-price-down close happened live (Anti-Rule 5), and RagingBull's undisclosed alongside-trading happened inside live sessions (Anti-Rule 3). The mechanic that builds legitimate retention and the mechanic that enables high-pressure conversion or an undisclosed conflict of interest are the same delivery format; what separates them is never selling on the call and never trading personally on-air without disclosure. Anti-Rule 11 states this explicitly for session content specifically.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** Technicals Desk should anchor on pre-London and pre-NY session-open commentary — the levels-to-watch generator already scoped in the roadmap — as a daily live ritual. AI Stack should anchor on a weekly live "Stack Heat Check" or congress-data read-through, turning already-planned rituals into scheduled, appointment-viewing events rather than posted threads.

### 7. A struggle-then-system origin story outperforms a polished-insider one — and it's the cheapest trust asset available

**Mechanism:** An origin story built on documented failure before the system was found lowers the trust bar for a skeptical beginner audience far more than credentials alone, because it implicitly promises "if I figured it out from here, you can follow the same path" — and it's lower-liability than a profit claim, since a story about the past doesn't imply a typical, replicable *outcome*.

**Evidence:**
- **Jordan Fogel / The Vault**'s origin story (tested every indicator imaginable, lost $15,000+, worked DoorDash to fund trading accounts, 2.5 unprofitable years) is the explicit backbone of a 25,000+-member free community.
- **Rayner Teo**'s "108kg fat kid who sucked at studies" framing is the lead line on his own site.
- **Mike Trades Daily**'s redemption arc (a $100 cannabis penny stock, WSB-style options gambling, four years of strategy-hopping before a journaling-driven system) is featured as a success story on two independent third-party sites specifically because of the struggle framing.
- Counterexample — **The Trading Cafe**'s founders are on record as non-traders/education entrepreneurs, an acknowledged authenticity gap the brand has to talk *around* rather than embody, even though it outscaled almost everyone using the struggle narrative (49k–97k members).

**Survivorship-bias check [added by adversarial audit]:** would a discredited brand's founder also tell a struggle-then-system story? Yes — Kyle Dennis's RagingBull persona and Umar Ashraf's "$20K into $18M" narrative (this document's own sharpest self-controlled-proof critique, Rule 2) are both struggle-to-riches origin stories, and both belong to two of the people this document treats most skeptically elsewhere. The narrative device predicts audience trust, not truthfulness or a lower failure rate — it works identically whether the underlying claim is honest or fabricated, which is exactly why it shows up on both sides of this research.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** The existing "couldn't afford Bloomberg, built my own terminal" story already has this shape. This research suggests naming the *specific* friction moment (a specific denied trial, a specific stale-data bad decision) the way Fogel names DoorDash and a $15K figure — a sensory-specific detail reads more credible than a general statement of struggle. **Guardrail:** only ever a true, verifiable detail. Inventing or dramatizing a specific moment for effect is the fake-proof/cosplay-identity failure mode this document flags elsewhere as the sharpest form of self-controlled, unverifiable "proof" (Rule 2) — a struggle story earns the same distrust as a P&L screenshot the moment it's caught being staged.

### 8. Narrow to one instrument, one setup, and brand the setup itself — especially at smaller scale

**Mechanism:** A sprawling curriculum is harder to differentiate and harder to prove out publicly; a single, named, repeatable setup traded live on a fixed schedule is both a content format and a walking advertisement, and it's realistically achievable for a solo operator without the large team or paid-acquisition budget the mega-brands used to scale.

**Evidence:**
- **Mike Trades Daily**'s entire brand is one instrument (NASDAQ/index futures) and one named setup ("Crazy Horse ORB"), reaching a functioning $147/mo Skool community (roughly 826 members — reported, a modeled third-party estimate per growyourskool.com's own stated "modeled-estimate methodology" in Part V, not a platform-disclosed figure; the false precision of "roughly 826" rather than "roughly 800" is itself a tell that this is a scraped point estimate, not a rounded approximation) at a scale a solo operator can plausibly run without a staffed team.
- **Warrior Trading** narrowed specifically to small-cap momentum day trading.
- **TJR** and **ICT** both center on Smart Money Concepts as one coherent lens rather than a general "everything about markets" curriculum.
- Counterexample — **Adam Khoo's Wealth Academy** deliberately spans stocks, options, and forex, but needed 20+ years of pre-existing fame and a self-improvement-author platform to sustain that breadth — not a realistic template for a new, single-operator brand.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** Technicals Desk should launch on one named, session-based setup built from the terminal's own session-level data — not a general TA curriculum — and broaden only after that setup has a public, timestamped track record, echoing Mike Trades Daily's scale rather than Adam Khoo's. **Guardrail [added by adversarial audit]:** build that track record the way the terminal already builds everything else — every call stamped and dated at the moment it's made, win or lose, published unedited — and the moment it is ever summarized as a win rate or a $/% figure, Anti-Rule 1's hypothetical-results disclaimer applies in full. A "public, timestamped track record" is exactly the artifact Warrior Trading's $3M FTC settlement shows can be entirely real and unedited and *still* be an actionable claim, if the marketing around it implies a typical, replicable outcome.

### 9. The visible subscription price is rarely the real economic engine — monetize downstream or adjacent, ideally into owned software

**Mechanism:** Trading/finance carries one of the highest median list prices on all of Skool (~$69/mo, platform-wide), yet several of the biggest brands enter members at $0–$45/month and make the real money in a separate, higher-ticket layer — a mentorship, an accelerator, or, in the single most structurally important pivot found in this research, a SaaS product sold to the same audience.

**Evidence:**
- **The Vault** and **The Trading Cafe** both keep the visible Skool tier free and run 8-week mentorships / "Quick Win" accelerators off-platform as the actual profit center.
- **Brad Goh's 1% Club** runs three monetization lines off one audience simultaneously — subscription, high-ticket mentorship, and a separate SaaS product (EdgeFlo).
- **Umar Ashraf**'s move from a ~$3,000 course (now widely reported defunct, with complaints of a dead community and lost refunds) to **TradeZella**, a functioning SaaS company profiled by Fast Company, is called "arguably the single most important strategic lesson in this whole cohort" — SaaS revenue doesn't require re-proving trading returns the way a course does.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** The terminal is the owner's own TradeZella-in-waiting — already half-built, already the roadmap's own "Phase 5 builder product" idea. The two Skool communities can function as the top-of-funnel/trust layer while a metered or tiered terminal-access product becomes the durable recurring line, decoupling revenue from having to keep proving trade calls. **Guardrail [added by adversarial audit]:** that decoupling applies to the *revenue model*, not the content standard — the daily brief and carousels still carry Anti-Rule 1's hypothetical-results discipline every single day regardless of which line item pays the bills. SaaS revenue removes the commercial pressure to inflate a claim; it does not remove the disclaimer requirement.

### 10. Turn members into case studies and co-instructors to scale credibility without scaling founder screen time

**Mechanism:** A founder's personal capacity to produce proof-of-work content is finite; showcasing members' documented wins (properly disclaimed) or promoting advanced members into teaching roles both multiplies content output and diversifies credibility away from a single person's track record — which also reduces single-point-of-failure risk if any one claim about the founder doesn't hold.

**Evidence:**
- **Timothy Sykes** explicitly promotes top students into instructors and free-lesson contributors, scaling his free-video library into the thousands without doing it all personally.
- **SMB Capital**'s free content functions as a recruiting funnel that identifies top performers for actual funded desk roles — the community's own success becomes the marketing.
- **Warrior Trading** and **Bear Bull Traders** both feature recurring member-success-story content.
- This pattern is explicitly flagged in the underlying YouTube research as "directly relevant to a two-community structure... cross-pollinating member wins between both communities."

**For the Engineer's Desk:** Build a lightweight, disclaimer-labeled "member desk notes" card format from day one — a member's (not the founder's) documented setup or fundamentals read gets featured, cross-posted between AI Stack and Technicals Desk so each community becomes proof-of-work for the other. The roadmap's own "member stock-request thread → next week's carousel" ritual is the seed of exactly this. **Guardrail [added by adversarial audit]:** keep the compensation one-directional — a featured member gets exposure/credit, never a commission, discount, or status tier tied to recruiting other paying members. The instant credit-for-content becomes payment-for-recruitment, this rule has walked into Anti-Rule 2's MLM structure wearing a content-marketing costume; IM Academy's compensation tier looked, from the member's side, exactly like "getting promoted for being good at this."

### 11. Run a deliberate multi-platform stack where each platform does exactly one job

**Mechanism:** No brand in this research relies on a single platform for everything; short-form (TikTok/IG/Shorts) does top-of-funnel reach and hooks, a chat layer (Discord/Telegram) does low-friction real-time access, and a structured platform (Skool, in nearly every close comparable) does the paid curriculum/gamification/community layer — collapsing these roles into one tool consistently underperforms the split.

**Evidence:**
- **TJR** runs TikTok/IG for reach, a free "TJR Freaks" community plus a paid Discord for access, and a separate application funnel for the high-ticket mentorship.
- **Ricky Gutierrez** already runs this exact split natively on Skool (multiple free communities plus a small paid tier) alongside his own site and Discord.
- **Tom Camp** layers TikTok, two Instagram handles, and a Telegram channel under a single $59/mo Skool tier.
- **Mike Trades Daily**'s entire structure is Instagram Reels (reach) feeding directly into Skool (paid, gamified layer) — the closest scale-match to a realistic starting point.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** Carousels/reels are the reach layer only; Skool — already the default platform for every closely-comparable brand here, including the identity-closest match, Bear Bull Traders — is the paid/gamified layer; the terminal is the retained product neither platform can replicate. Keep the three roles distinct rather than trying to sell terminal access inside a caption.

### 12. A written, long-form asset compounds distribution that video alone can't reach

**Mechanism:** Video platforms are rented attention that resets with every algorithm change; a written asset (blog, book, structured brief) is indexable, citable, and accumulates SEO and reference value over years, reaching a "quiet reader" segment that would never find the brand via video — and it's dramatically cheaper to produce at volume than daily video.

**Evidence:**
- **Bear Bull Traders**' Andrew Aziz built his funnel's earliest stage on a bestselling book (Amazon distribution, reviews, SEO) called "structurally different from and complementary to" YouTube.
- **Rayner Teo** backs 845 videos with a 35+-guide free blog functioning as a parallel SEO engine.
- **SMB Capital**'s Mike Bellafiore is a traditionally published author (*One Good Trade*, Wiley; *The PlayBook*, FT/Pearson), leveraged into CNBC/Bloomberg/Fox Business coverage — institutional proof no pure video creator in this set can match.
- Counterexample — neither **The Trading Cafe** nor **TJR** shows meaningful evidence of this asset class, and both compensate with heavier paid-acquisition spend (landing pages, Facebook ads, clip farms) instead.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** The daily brief is already this asset. Treat it as a compounding, indexable archive — a running, dated library — rather than a disposable daily Story, since it's the lowest-marginal-cost content the terminal produces and the one most likely to outlive any single platform's algorithm shift.

### 13. Anchor the entry price low, then climb the ladder transparently — a steep, undisclosed jump reads as bait-and-switch

**Mechanism:** A very low or symbolic entry price (even $1) converts hesitant browsers by removing signup friction while still creating enough sunk-cost commitment to drive engagement — but this only works long-term when subsequent tiers are visible, graduated steps, not a single enormous, opaque jump revealed only after signup.

**Evidence:**
- **Trading Tribe**'s $1/mo entry is "a psychological-commitment device more than a revenue line," but its jump toward a reported five-figure "1% Club" backend is flagged as "a steep anchor jump that can read as bait-and-switch."
- **Bear Bull Traders** instead runs the most granular, SaaS-style graduated ladder found in this research — $39 trial → $99/mo → $199/mo → $1,099/yr — each step visible and self-selected.
- **Mike Trades Daily / Midas Touch**'s $47 → $97 → $147 tiers are explicitly built as "a progression/status system," not a bait step. **[Internal contradiction flagged by this audit, unresolved by the original synthesis:** Rule 14 below cites this same live-trading-room price as "rising from $47 toward $130," not $147 — the two figures cannot both be the ceiling, and it isn't even clear from the underlying sources whether "Midas Touch" is the same product as Mike Trades Daily's core Skool community or a distinct live-room sub-brand. Treat both numbers as unverified until re-checked against a live listing before either appears in published material.]
- Counterexample — **Freedom Team Trading**'s off-platform mentorship pricing, gated entirely behind an application call with no published number, is flagged as reading "evasive."

**For the Engineer's Desk:** Price both communities as visible, named steps from the outset — a low-friction community tier, a mid-tier unlocking deeper terminal data, a top tier with live session participation — rather than gating the top price behind a sales call. An engineer publishes the spec sheet; he doesn't negotiate in a closed room.

### 14. Real, platform-native scarcity converts; manufactured urgency is both a trust-killer and a regulatory tripwire

**Mechanism:** A price that genuinely rises as a fixed-capacity room fills is a truthful signal a buyer can verify by watching the counter. A countdown timer or "X spots left" claim that isn't tied to a real, checkable constraint is the same urgency mechanic wearing a costume — and the Failure File shows regulators treat aggressive, undisclosed urgency tactics as evidence of deception in their own right, independent of whether the underlying product is legitimate.

**Evidence:**
- **Midas Touch**'s live-trading room price is reported rising from $47 toward $130 as real headcount climbs toward a stated 200-member cap — "textbook urgency pricing implemented as a platform feature," though even this is flagged as something "sophisticated buyers may discount as manufactured scarcity" if the cap itself isn't verifiable. (Note: Rule 13 above cites this same brand's tiers as topping out at $147, not $130 — the two figures conflict and neither was reconciled by this synthesis; re-verify before citing either number.)
- **Trading Tribe**'s "1% Accelerator" runs a similar real, rising pre-launch price toward $1,997.
- Counterexample — **Online Trading Academy**'s negotiate-the-price-down live sales pattern ("$23,000, then $21,000, then $17,000") was cited as consumer evidence supporting a $362M FTC judgment; the anti-rule is to never run this pattern regardless of product quality (Part II, #5).

**For the Engineer's Desk:** If a live-room tier is ever capacity-limited, make the cap a real, published number tied to something checkable — never an arbitrary countdown — and never negotiate a published community price down on a call, consistent with the roadmap's own "never discount, add perks instead" pricing discipline.

---

### 15. Build stickiness through named ritual and vocabulary, not through punishing dissent

**Mechanism:** The same in-group mechanics that make a vocabulary spread fast — repetition, shared language, ritual, belonging — sit structurally adjacent to the mechanics that turn a community into an echo chamber where skepticism reads as betrayal. These are the same lever pulled two different ways, and "criticism gets banned instantly, the guru becomes untouchable" is independently named the #1 red flag across multiple fake-guru teardowns.

**Evidence:**
- **ICT**-adjacent communities are described in multiple independent sources as having a "cult-like atmosphere where adherents dismiss anyone not following [the] methods" — the same orthodoxy that gave the vocabulary transmission fidelity is the exact thing critics point to as the tell.
- **Trading for Women**'s structured "Compound Plan" tracker and Skool's own points/levels/leaderboards (used explicitly by **Just Simple Trading** and TFW) show the identical belonging-mechanic can be built through gamification and shared ritual instead of enforced conformity.

**For the Engineer's Desk:** Build the named vocabulary and live rituals (Part IV) as teaching tools members opt into, paired with an explicit, written moderation policy stating skeptical questions are welcome and only harassment/spam is bannable. Stickiness should come from shared language and live rituals — never from silencing a member who asks "does this actually backtest." **Note [added by adversarial audit]:** this rule leaves a tension elsewhere in the document unresolved. ICT is cited approvingly in Rule 3 (gave the method away free) and Rule 5 (vocabulary compounded for free), and cited as the cautionary example right here for "cult-like" orthodoxy-enforcement — the same brand is this research's best evidence for one move and its clearest warning against another. Copy the free-method-and-vocabulary mechanics; do not copy the orthodoxy-enforcement culture. The two are separable, and nothing in Rules 3 or 5 should be read as an endorsement of ICT wholesale just because two of fifteen rules cite it favorably.

## Part II — The anti-rules

Pulled directly from the Failure File's regulatory/reputational case ledger. Every one of these is tied to a specific, sourced case — none are hypothetical.

1. **Never advertise a specific $/% outcome without a hypothetical-results disclaimer and a written record substantiating it.** Warrior Trading's *"$101,280.47 in Verified Profits"* and *"$300,000 in four days"* claims anchored a $3M FTC settlement (2022) even though the underlying trades were real — the violation was implying a typical, replicable outcome, not lying about the trade.
2. **Never build a compensation tier where members earn by recruiting other paying members.** IM Academy / iMarketsLive's MLM structure produced an alleged $1.2B consumer-harm case and a $795.8M judgment against its ringleaders (2025–26), on top of an ignored 2018 CFTC warning shot.
3. **Never let an instructor trade in front of, or alongside, the calls being taught without a disclosed firewall.** Jason Bond, Jeffrey Bishop, and Kyle Dennis at RagingBull.com were accused of exactly this inside a $137M FTC action; Kyle Dennis alone drew a permanent injunction barring earnings claims without written substantiation.
4. **Never post an unverifiable on-screen "win" as social proof.** The fake-P&L genre (India's "Stock Burner" exposé; a Polymarket "win"-video investigation reportedly finding none of 1,100+ reviewed videos were real, ~$1.9M in staged wins — reported; note that despite the "WSJ investigation" framing this bullet originally carried, no wsj.com URL actually appears anywhere in Part V's "every URL cited" source list for this figure, only squaredtech.co and a Yahoo Finance write-up, both labeled (reported) there — re-verify the outlet and the number before repeating either) and the fake-lifestyle genre (Daniel Savage's rented Ferraris; "Mr. Keyz" caught live asking his father for a Mercedes' keys — both reported, sourced only to third-party exposé blogs in Part V, not to primary footage) exist specifically to forensically debunk this move — the debunk outlives and eventually outranks the original.
5. **Never run a negotiate-the-price-down live sales close.** Online Trading Academy's "$23,000, then $21,000, then $17,000" pattern was cited as consumer evidence in a $362M FTC judgment; the pattern itself, independent of product quality, reads as deceptive.
6. **Never assume a dismissed case means the brand walked away unscathed.** Both the SEC/DOJ Discord/Twitter-influencer case (criminal indictment dismissed March 2024) and CFTC v. MyForexFunds (dismissed with sanctions recommended against the CFTC itself, 2025) show the freeze/indictment moment — not the final verdict — is what kills a business's assets and trust.
7. **Never run the payments stack without watching the chargeback rate.** Course-industry refund requests reportedly run 18–28% of sales, and a processor's dispute threshold (reported at 0.75% for Stripe by a third-party chargeback guide cited in Part V, not Stripe's own published policy page — re-verify against Stripe's current terms before treating it as fixed) triggers freezes, reserves, and a 5-year MATCH-list blacklist faster than any regulator could act. Skool itself routes refund decisions back to the creator, not the platform.
8. **Never let community culture treat skepticism as an attack.** "Criticism gets banned instantly, the guru becomes untouchable" is independently named the #1 red flag across multiple fake-guru teardowns, and it's the same orthodoxy-enforcement mechanic that gets ICT-adjacent communities described as "cult-like" in public forums.
9. **Never name a real, identifiable rival as the villain, even implicitly.** No case in the Failure File traces to this specific mistake, because every brand studied already avoids it — the owner's own framing (gatekept institutional tooling, hustle-guru culture, no named individuals) is the structurally safer choice precisely because it can't be defamation-tested or turned into a rival's rebuttal moment.
10. **Never leave a sponsorship, affiliate link, or paid placement undisclosed inside educational content.** Undisclosed paid promotion (DISTX, HEX, SAFEMOON) is a named, recurring thread in the BitBoy Crypto / Ben Armstrong collapse and is independently an FTC endorsement-guide violation regardless of whatever else is happening.
11. **Never frame session-level output as a buy/sell signal, trade call, or personalized recommendation — structure and levels-to-watch only.** This is the boundary that separates a data terminal from an alert/signal-selling service, and it is exactly what CFTC Rule 4.41's hypothetical-performance framing and NFA Interpretive Notice 9025's promotional-material standard exist to police (both sourced in Part V's Method-brand segment but never surfaced as an operating rule until this one). Part IV's Candidate 2 already names the right posture ("structure, not signals") as a rationale aside; this item promotes it to a governing rule. The same discipline binds any live session (Rule 6): narrating a level is compliant, narrating "I'm long here, you should be too" is not, and the distance between those two sentences is the entire distance between Technicals Desk and RagingBull's undisclosed-alongside-trading anti-rule (#3 above). *[Added by adversarial audit — this rule was fully evidenced by Part V's own sources but had never been written down.]*

**Two structural notes worth carrying forward:** the FCA prosecutes based on a post's promotional *framing and reach*, not the poster's stated educational intent (relevant the moment either community picks up UK/EU members); and an entire content vertical can be deplatformed or demonetized because of bad actors elsewhere in the category — Facebook/Google's 2018 binary-options/crypto ad bans and TikTok's current exclusion of forex/CFD/crypto content from its Branded Content program are both category-level risk, independent of the owner's own conduct.

## Part III — The composite playbook

One coherent build sequence, assembled from the strongest pattern in each segment (Skool = funnel/retention infrastructure, YouTube = trust/credibility mechanics, short-form = distribution/hooks, method+failures = vocabulary design + guardrails), written against the owner's real assets: the terminal (`web/`, `scripts/`), the carousel/capture-card generator (`higgs/`), the daily brief generator (`content/daily_brief/`), the planned session-recap generator for London/NY opens, and the congress + capital-web data feed.

**Step 1 — Identity, before anything ships.** Lock two separate identity filters and treat them as a TFW-style fork of one engine, not one brand wearing two hats. AI Stack: *"the engineer-investor who wants Bloomberg-grade fundamentals without the Bloomberg bill or the CNBC noise."* Technicals Desk: *"the day-job trader who needs session-scoped setups that fit around a 9-to-5, not a full trading-floor screen commitment"* — the Bear Bull Traders match, made explicit. Both share one root story: gatekept tooling, so he built his own — never a named villain (Rule 4), always a specific friction detail, not a general complaint (Rule 7). **[Clarification added by adversarial audit]:** "identity filter" throughout this document means who the content is aimed at and written for — it is not license to fabricate a second on-screen persona (a fictional "day-job trader" host distinct from the actual owner) the way a lifestyle-flex brand fabricates a net worth. Both communities are the same real person addressing two real audiences; that has to stay true even as the framing and tone fork, or this step quietly becomes a cosplay-identity failure mode — no single anti-rule in Part II names persona fabrication directly, which is itself a gap in this synthesis worth closing if either community ever brings on a second on-screen host; Anti-Rule 4's fake-lifestyle cases are the closest analogue, since a fabricated persona is the same move as a fabricated Ferrari, just aimed at relatability instead of wealth.

**Step 2 — Free content engine: daily, complete, not a teaser.** The daily brief and capture cards become the ungated, complete free product (Rule 3), posted with short-form discipline: a dollar/data-specific opening line in the first seconds, a 3–5×/week floor minimum — realistically daily, since the terminal auto-generates the underlying numbers. Every card carries a visible timestamp/source stamp (Rule 2) baked into the design itself, not a footnote. Congress-trade and capital-web content run at the cadence already set in the roadmap (1–2×/week) — the highest raw-engagement hook neither Skool-native nor YouTube-native competitors in this research can generate on demand.

**Step 3 — Named method and vocabulary (candidates in Part IV).** Once the free engine has run long enough to show which cards and lessons repeat and resonate, formalize the recurring concepts into a small vocabulary anchored to real, computed fields — never chart narrative alone (Rule 5 + Rule 2 combined). This is the layer that turns "Stack Heat Check" and "levels to watch" from post titles into a teachable, ownable method other people eventually borrow — which, per Rule 5, is the growth engine, not a leak to plug.

**Step 4 — Free community: Skool, ungated, gamified, live from day one.** Launch each community's free tier on Skool specifically because it is already the default platform for every closely-comparable brand in this research (Ricky Gutierrez, Mike Trades Daily, Tom Camp, and — by audience type — Bear Bull Traders), and its native points/levels/leaderboards do real retention work for free (Rule 6 + Rule 15). Publish the moderation policy — skeptical questions welcome, only harassment/spam bannable — on day one, before the first controversial question ever arrives. **[Added by adversarial audit]:** Skool is global by default — the moment either community's member list shows UK/EU signups, the FCA's framing-and-reach standard (Part II's structural notes) applies regardless of intent. Decide the geographic posture — geoblock, a jurisdiction disclaimer, or a compliance review — before that first international member joins, not after; the original build sequence named this risk in Part II but never gave it an operational step here.

**Step 5 — Paid ladder: visible steps, terminal access at the top.** Anchor low (a symbolic or near-symbolic entry price, Rule 13), one or two visible middle tiers unlocking deeper terminal data (screener access, on-request dossiers, the session-level archive), and a top tier bundling live participation. Never gate the top price behind an application call — the Vault's flagged weakness. Over the following two to four quarters, deliberately decouple revenue from having to keep proving trade calls by metering terminal access itself as the durable recurring line (Rule 9) — the roadmap's own "builder product" idea, arrived at here by evidence rather than instinct. **[Added by adversarial audit]:** publish that metered-terminal tier as a named, dated line on the roadmap from launch day — even if only as "coming Q_" — rather than introducing it as a surprise two-to-four-quarter follow-on. Rule 13's own evidence is explicit that an undisclosed later jump, even a well-intentioned one, reads exactly like Trading Tribe's flagged $1/mo-to-five-figure pattern; this step as originally written risked doing the thing Rule 13 warns against three steps earlier in the same document.

**Step 6 — Retention rituals: live, scheduled, cross-pollinated.** Daily pre-London/pre-NY session-open commentary for Technicals Desk; a weekly live "Stack Heat Check" or congress-data read for AI Stack (Rule 6). Layer in a recurring, disclaimer-labeled "member desk notes" card format that cross-posts documented member setups between both communities (Rule 10), so each community becomes visible proof-of-work for the other without adding founder screen time — the single highest-leverage move available once both communities exist, per the underlying YouTube research's explicit call-out of this exact two-community structure.

## Part IV — Named method opportunity

Two candidate vocabularies, one per community, plus a shared umbrella term — all designed to answer Rule 5's sharpest constraint: terms should resolve to something the terminal can actually compute and stamp, not a narrative chart-read, which is a structural improvement over the vocabulary critique leveled at both ICT and Elliott Wave.

### Candidate 1 — "The Stack-Layer Method" (AI Stack)

Brand the five-layer taxonomy already encoded in this repo's own investing brief — Energy → Chips → Infra/Neocloud → Models → Application — as the lens every stock gets run through, with a small set of coined sub-terms that each resolve to a computed field already in the pipeline:
- **Layer Capture** — the share of a value chain's economics a company visibly captures at its layer (computable from margin/multiple data already pulled).
- **Stack Depth** — how many layers a single name touches (a diversification/concentration read).
- **Choke Point** — a name sitting at a structural bottleneck within its layer.
- **Layer Bleed** — value visibly migrating from one layer to an adjacent one across a reporting period (a comparable-period multiple/margin shift).

**Rationale:** this directly answers the Method segment's sharpest critique of ICT/Elliott Wave vocabulary — every term resolves to a number the terminal already computes and stamps with `retrieved_at`/`source`, making it falsifiable and reproducible rather than narrative, and it reuses infrastructure that already exists (the stack framing is already the roadmap's niche statement) rather than inventing new IP from nothing.

### Candidate 2 — "The Clock-Out Method" (Technicals Desk)

A session-based vocabulary explicitly designed around trading inside a day job's real windows, in contrast to ICT's "kill zones," which presume a trader is at the screen full-time:
- **Desk Open** — the levels captured at a session's open (London/NY), the direct product of the planned session-recap generator.
- **Clock-In Check** — a five-minute pre-market/pre-shift level review.
- **Clock-Out Review** — an end-of-day trade/level review ritual, echoing the journaling discipline Mike Trades Daily explicitly teaches as part of his system rather than as a separate habit.

**Rationale:** no brand profiled across any of the four underlying reports names a method after the day-job constraint itself — every "session"/"kill zone" framing in this research assumes full-time or near-full-time screen availability. Naming the method after the actual constraint the target member has (a day job) is a genuine, evidenced-absent gap in this market, and it fuses the identity filter and the vocabulary into one ownable phrase (Rule 1 + Rule 5), while its explicit "structure, not signals" framing keeps it inside the roadmap's own higher compliance bar for Community B.

### Candidate 3 — "Terminal-Grade" / "The Build Sheet" (shared umbrella)

Rather than merging the two communities' vocabularies, give them one shared house-style descriptor and one shared content template both recognize even though their named methods differ: **"terminal-grade"** as the recurring quality claim ("terminal-grade data, not a screenshot"), and **"The Build Sheet"** as the name for the structured, spec-sheet-style card format — inputs, computed fields, timestamp, source — that every carousel uses regardless of which community it's for.

**Rationale:** Bear Bull Traders and SMB Capital both show that an engineering/institutional register (published research, a real operating desk) reads as more durable trust than a personality-brand register, even though it likely caps the realistic subscriber ceiling versus flashier competitors — a pattern independently observed in the YouTube research. "Terminal-grade" / "The Build Sheet" leans into that tradeoff on purpose: it matches the owner's actual professional identity rather than borrowing a hustle-culture register, and the Failure File shows that register carries the most regulatory and reputational risk in this entire market.
